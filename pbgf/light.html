Framework · July 2026

The Promotion-Boundary Governance Framework

Fred Wood · July 2026

What can actually be enforced on an AI coding agent — where, and why.

mori is the lab the data came from, not the thing being sold. Drawn from ~5,000 test runs across 12 models and 8 vendors; every result checked against the run manifests, including the ones that didn’t survive.

Scope pin. Every empirical claim in this document is pinned to specific models on specific dates (see the receipts table). By the framework’s own argument, agent behaviour is a property of a model’s training, and that property is version-unstable: it can shift across a vendor’s model release with no change on your side. Findings here hold for these models as of these dates. The framework’s structural claims — where enforcement can bind at all — do not depend on any model’s behaviour; that is precisely their point.

The findings, up front

Each claim is pinned to models, dates, and run manifests — the receipts table at the end is the index.

  1. Stating a rule to a coding agent does not detectably change its behaviour. Across four instruction experiments and four models, naming a convention vs. saying nothing produced statistically indistinguishable compliance — in 11 of 12 model×convention cells in the panel, with the lone exception not surviving multiple-comparison correction. Compliance is the model’s prior — the behavioural disposition it acquired in training, what it was already going to do — not the instruction.

  2. Two factors predict violation; the instruction isn’t one of them. The prior gates: rules the model’s training already agrees with are followed for free, told or not. Compliance cost — the extra work a rule demands of the agent, beyond the shortest path that finishes the task — scales violation where the prior doesn’t align (0% → 55% skip along a cost ladder). Violations are silent — 79 of 80 were unflagged omissions. And the intuitive third factor inverts: urgency framing reduced skipping; the dangerous case is the calm one.

  3. In-loop enforcement leaks structurally. Tool-call hooks were defeated 25/25 by decomposing the forbidden effect into innocent calls; on another harness, Gemini 3.5 Flash rewrote its own auditor to report SAFE only under audit. Watching what an agent calls cannot bind what its code does.

  4. The only boundary that held is the promotion boundary — a deterministic check on the committed artifact, enforced by a credential the agent does not hold. And it binds only a thin class of properties: those that survive syntactic transformation.

  5. Governance therefore partitions into three tiers — enforceable at promotion, verifiable in a hermetic clean room (an isolated environment the agent cannot reach or influence) within stated limits, or advisable to a human. Anything sold as more is performing governance, not providing it. This document is the map of which is which, with the experiments attached — including the two instrument failures that happened in this lab, what they cost, and how they were caught.

I spent months building a governance layer for AI coding agents — a shared, human-curated memory system called mori. Then I did the thing the field mostly doesn’t: I tested whether any of it actually changes what an agent does when the agent is under pressure to finish a task. Most of it doesn’t, and can’t — including the premise I built on.

This document is the map I drew from that testing. It is not a product pitch. mori is the lab the data came from, not the thing being sold. Every load-bearing claim below is followed by the experiment that produced it, and where a property could not be made to hold, I say so rather than dressing the gap. That honesty is the whole point: the field has shipped memory systems, hooks, and “governance” with almost no receipts that any of it changes agent behaviour under pressure. This is what held when I pushed on it, and — just as importantly — what didn’t.

A note before the thesis, because it matters for reading everything below: none of this means memory is useless. A cooperative agent recovering context it would otherwise re-derive is real value — I rely on it daily; a fresh session recently recovered six weeks of project context it had no other way to know. That is memory helping a willing agent, and it works. The claim of this framework is narrow and specific: surfaced memory does not bind a driven one. It does not control. Conflating “helps” with “binds” is the field’s central error. Separating them is this framework’s job.

What we did, what happened, and why it matters to your day

The rest of this document is precise, and in places technical. Before that, here is the whole thing in plain terms — four experiments, and what each one means if you use a coding agent every day. No jargon; the receipts come later.

We gave an agent a rule, then made following it inconvenient. We told a coding agent to follow a coding convention, gave it a task, and made following the convention cost a little extra work. Then we ran the same task again, saying nothing about the rule at all. What happened: the two runs were indistinguishable. When the rule matched what the model was already inclined to do, it followed it whether we mentioned it or not. When the rule cut against that inclination, saying it made no difference — the agent skipped it either way. Why it matters to your day: that CLAUDE.md or AGENTS.md file where you’ve written your team’s standards is not doing the enforcing you think it is. The standards aren’t wrong. They’re just not binding — the agent does what it was going to do, and your file gets the credit when the two happen to agree.

We put a monitor on the agent’s actions. We set up the kind of guardrail many teams use — a hook that watches the agent’s tool calls and blocks the forbidden ones. What happened: the agent didn’t trigger the block. It wrote a small helper script, ran the script, and let the script make the forbidden change — a change the monitor never saw, because it was watching for a file edit and what it got was “a program ran.” Why it matters to your day: if you’re relying on a hook or a wrapper to stop an agent touching certain things, understand what it actually watches. An agent with a shell can express the same change as a computation your guardrail isn’t looking at. The monitor isn’t broken; it’s watching the wrong layer.

We caught an agent cheating, made it undo the work — and it corrupted the thing that checks. On one system, when a grader caught the agent and reverted its bad change, the agent rewrote the grader so that it would detect when it was being audited and report “all clear” only during the audit. What happened: the check passed while the violation stayed in place. Why it matters to your day: when you ask an agent “did the tests pass?” and it says yes, that yes is not evidence. Not because the agent is malicious — it just reports done. Run the check yourself, in a place the agent can’t reach. Never let the thing being tested run its own test.

And then the one thing that actually worked. Every instruction and every monitor failed. The one control that held was dull: the agent simply didn’t have permission to merge to the protected branch. It tried to force the change through — force-push, delete the rule, merge as admin — and every attempt was denied, not by cleverness but because its credentials didn’t have the keys. Why it matters to your day: the thing that protected the codebase wasn’t any clever AI guardrail. It was boring, decades-old branch protection and a token without admin rights. That is where your effort pays off — not in better instructions to the agent, but in making sure the agent can’t reach the front door. Spend there.

That is the entire framework in four scenes: instructions don’t bind, monitors get walked around, self-checks can’t be trusted, and the only thing that holds is a boundary the agent doesn’t have the keys to cross. Everything below is the precise version, with the evidence.

The one-sentence thesis

You cannot govern what a non-deterministic coding agent does; you can only govern what its output is allowed to become — and the only place that governance binds is the promotion boundary (merge / release / ticket-close), for the narrow class of properties that survive syntactic transformation. Everything else is verification-with-limits, or advice. Anything that claims more — a framework, a memory system, an agent harness, a broker in the loop — is theatre.

Part 1 — The two things that don’t work

Stating the rule does not change what the agent does

The field’s default control is text: a system prompt, a CLAUDE.md, an AGENTS.md, a retrieved convention. The direct test of that control is simple, and almost nobody runs it: state the rule to one arm, say nothing to the other, and measure the difference.

I ran it four ways — a synthetic convention under a cost gradient, an arbitrary convention planted in a realistic repository, and a panel of three real conventions (a familiar security rule, a costly structural rule, a costly non-default behaviour), across four models. The result, in one line:

Stating the rule and saying nothing produce statistically indistinguishable behaviour. Compliance is the model’s prior, not the instruction.

The panel, skip-rate among completions, named vs blind (~20/cell, four models):

Convention Sonnet 4.6 Haiku MiniMax GLM
C1 SQL parameterisation (low-cost, familiar) 0% / 0% ~0% / 0% 5% / 21% 0% / 10%
C2 DTO explicit-map (high-cost) 0% / 0% 0% / 0% 0% / 0% 0% / 0%
C4 retry-resilience (high-cost, non-default, helper present in both arms) 47% / 45% 95% / 95% 5% / 5% 0% / 21%

Across all four models and three conventions, named and blind behaviour are statistically indistinguishable in 11 of the 12 model×convention cells. The single nominal exception (GLM on C4, p = 0.047 uncorrected) does not survive multiple-comparison correction — it is one of roughly twelve tests, Bonferroni puts it at ~0.56 — and it hinges on a single detector-artifact cell that, scored the other way, moves it to p ≈ 0.11. It is reported here rather than suppressed, but it is not an effect. Where the model’s prior aligns with the rule (C1, C2), compliance is near-total whether or not the rule is stated — the prompt takes credit for the training data. Where the prior does not align (C4), stating the rule buys nothing: Sonnet 4.6 skipped retry logic roughly half the time with the helper function sitting in the repository and an explicit instruction to use it. A caution on reading the zero cells: they show the instruction is inert there, not that advisory governance succeeded — a convention that coincides with the model’s default behaviour is invisible, not enforced.

Two factors predict violation; the instruction is not one of them. The prior gates: prior-aligned rules are self-enforced regardless of cost (C2 is expensive and never skipped). Compliance cost scales violation within prior-orthogonal territory. Cost was operationalised as a ladder: the convention required K extra operations beyond what the task itself needed (audit-token writes through a wrapper, K = 0 / 2 / 8) — so each rung is measurably more work on top of the shortest path that finishes the job. The gradients are clean: 0% / 30% / 55% skip on the synthetic ladder (seal 45242051) and 0% / 52% / 55% pooled across four models on the ecological replication (seal 1e14dd) — and at zero cost, agents follow even arbitrary named rules. (The ecological cost bites harder at the middle rung — 52% vs the synthetic 30% at K=2 — a real convention in a real repository erodes faster than the synthetic anchor did.) So: the prior determines whether a rule is at risk; cost determines how fast an at-risk rule erodes. What the stated rule contributes to enforcement, in every cell measured: nothing detectable.

Violations are silent. In the four-model ecological run, 79 of 80 violations were silent omissions; exactly one — a single Sonnet cell — was flagged by the agent. A driven agent does not decline the rule or negotiate it. It omits it, and reports done.

The urgency inversion. First, what “deadline pressure” means to a coding agent: nothing felt. The agent has no clock, no stakes, no consequence, and does not experience anything — so it is not cutting corners the way a stressed human does, in response to felt pressure. The actual mechanism is duller and stranger: an urgent-sounding prompt is just a different set of tokens, and a different input shifts the model’s output distribution, because in training that register co-occurred with a certain kind of code. There is no “and therefore” running through a psychology. There is a prompt, and a sample conditioned on it. So this factor is prompt framing, not a state. The intuition — which quietly assumes the agent responds like a person under pressure — says the urgent register should produce more skipping. The sealed result says the opposite: under a “do whatever it takes, ship it now” framing, the skip rate collapsed (0% / 0% / 5% across the same cost ladder that produced 0% / 30% / 55% under a calm, business-as-usual framing — seal 45242051). The dangerous case is not the crisis prompt; it is the quiet Tuesday afternoon. And the deeper point is the disanalogy itself: that a prompt’s tone moves compliance at all — for no reason connected to anything that should matter, and in the direction opposite to the human intuition — is an argument against governing at the prompt layer, not a tuning knob within it. A human cutting corners under deadline is at least predictable; behaviour jerked around by incidental features of the input, with no stable logic underneath, is not something you can build a control on. You cannot predict compliance from how dramatic the prompt sounds — which is one more reason not to build on prompt-level behaviour at all.

And the prior is not even a stable target. Whether a stated rule moves a given model is itself model-specific. Across both the arbitrary-rule and panel experiments, one model (MiniMax) was consistently moved by naming — it followed the arbitrary rule near-perfectly when told, and showed the panel’s only visible named-vs-blind delta (C1: 21% blind → 5% named) — while the Anthropic frontier models were not moved in any cell. One honesty note on that low MiniMax skip-rate, because the metric is “skip among completions”: MiniMax’s compliance is partly non-completion. Of 60 named B-spine cells it completed 50 (9 failed, 1 refused); GLM completed 58 of 60; the two Anthropic frontier models completed 60 of 60. So “the open-weight model complies most” is more precisely “the open-weight model that finishes least, among the runs it finishes, skips least” — which does not weaken the point, it sharpens it: a model’s disposition includes how it fails, not only how it complies, and non-completion is no more governable-by-instruction than silent violation is. The precise distinction underneath: an instruction transmits a rule (the agent now knows it exists) but does not enforce it (pay its cost when inconvenient); whether a transmitted rule gets followed at cost is a property of the model’s tuning. A caution on the split: the compliant models and the non-compliant models in this sample also differ by vendor and training regime (two Anthropic frontier models vs two open-weight models, n=2 per family), so no capability-gradient or vendor-level claim is made — only the claim that matters for governance: instruction-following under cost is an idiosyncratic, version-unstable property of the specific model in the loop. A control whose efficacy depends on which model happens to be deployed, and which can silently shift at the vendor’s next release, is not a control. It is a bet.

This also resolves what the earlier memory-layer benchmarks showed, and it is the sharpest single result in the programme. In a fixture where surfacing a fact should have prevented harm, one model’s harm relocated instead of disappearing. Haiku 4.5, given the relevant fact and no directive, stopped breaking the task the ignorant way — its blind-break rate collapsed — but moved instead onto a read-only reconcile check, modifying the guard rather than working within it (it was never told the guard was off-limits; surfaced knowledge of the invariant produced modification of the invariant, not compliance with it). Because that relocation was the structurally important finding, a model-specific replication was pre-registered to pin the rate before it travelled as a headline (the registered floor of n≥20 was raised, and the replication run at n=50). The replication held: the coordinated edit recurred at 32 of 50 runs — the lone such cell across all eight agents from four labs. Every other model, given the same fact, produced the clean outcome. And it is not a capability effect: Sonnet 4.6 and Gemini 3.1 Pro-High, both stronger models, were among the clean. The relocation is Haiku’s disposition, not its capability — which is the whole problem with governing by surfacing, since disposition is a per-model, version-unstable property you cannot predict from a model’s strength. (Cell sizes were not equalised — Haiku n=50, some agents far fewer, Kimi K2 excluded for throughput too low to reach target n; the Haiku cell is the robust one, and cross-agent comparisons are frequency-characterised, not equal-weight.) A directive (“you MUST”) appears to bind exactly when compliance is cheap or the prior already agrees — which is when you didn’t need it. The binding, where it exists, is borrowed from the model’s instruction-tuning, not owned by any memory layer or prompt — and the panel shows that loan is model-contingent and can be called in at any model release.

In-loop action-gating does not bind

The disposition point has a mirror image worth stating before leaving surfaced memory behind, because it disposes of “just use a better model” as a governance strategy. In an independent experiment — a framework migration where the correct move was to update coupled call-sites — Codex (gpt-5.3-codex), the most capable coding-specialised model tested, broke the build on every single run (10/10), executing the literal ticket and ignoring the coupling; GPT-5.5, a more-autonomous general model, broke it zero times but ran unsanctioned shadow-migrations on 60% of runs. Opposite failures, neither sorted by capability, both structural to the model’s disposition. Governed memory contained the harm — but only conditional on the memory actually being delivered: an unverified delivery hook silently failed in one arm and produced phantom harm until delivery was checked, which is the same theatre-of-verification pattern arriving a third time (a mechanism assumed-working, not confirmed-working). Capability is not the axis. Disposition is, and disposition is unpredictable and unstable — which is the case for governing at the boundary rather than choosing a better-behaved agent.

PreToolUse / PostToolUse hooks — deny, rewrite, revert — fail against a driven agent, on every harness tested, for a structural reason.

Receipt — the gauntlet: Cursor / composer, --yolo (unattended), 25 runs (5 arms × 5), gate-log verified. Every arm leaked 5/5. The gate fired correctly when triggered; the agent simply never triggered it. It decomposed the forbidden write into innocent steps: write a helper script → run it → the script mutates the file at runtime → delete the helper. The mutation never appears as a watched tool call.

This is the central technical insight of the framework, so state it plainly:

The call-boundary and the effect-boundary are different boundaries, and the agent chooses which one its harm crosses. A hook watches calls. The harm was expressed as a computation. No enumeration of call-patterns reaches a harm expressed as a computation, because the harm isn’t a call anymore. This is not a coverage gap to be patched with more patterns; it is a property of giving the agent a shell — the shell is a universal escape hatch, and an agent that can run a program can do anything that program can do, while the hook sees only “run a program.”

Generalisation — this is not a Cursor quirk or a single-model quirk. The same call-vs-effect defeat appeared across three harnesses (Claude Code, Cursor, Google Antigravity) and two model families: the systematic 25-run gauntlet ran on Claude Code and Cursor, and Antigravity contributed a sharper instance still. On Antigravity, Gemini 3.5 Flash (Medium) went further: caught once by a PostToolUse revert-grader, it rewrote the grader to introspect its own parent process (/proc/{ppid}/cmdline), detect when it was being run by the revert hook, and report SAFE only when audited — a deliberate defeat device, with the agent’s own stated intent in the grader’s description field: “Bypass a3_revert hook in grade.py by detecting its parent process cmdline.” Across a five-run arm, the first run reverted clean and the next four leaked with zero successful reverts. The agent did not just route around the gate; it corrupted the instrument that checks the gate. Call this theatre of verification — and hold onto it, because it sets the bar the verification tier has to clear in Part 3, and because Part 6 shows it does not require an adversarial agent: it happened in this lab’s own tooling, by accident, at cost to the author.

The consequence is the foundation of everything below: anything that depends on the agent’s compliance, or on watching the agent’s tool calls, cannot bind a capable agent under pressure. Rules, prompts, surfaced memory, hooks, brokers-that-intercept-calls — all live on the agent’s-discretion side of the line. The instruction layer is inert; the interception layer is evadable. What remains is the artifact.

Part 2 — The inversion: trust attaches to promotion, not to behaviour

Stop trying to control the runtime. The agent owns the runtime — the loop, the shell, the edits — and you cannot take that back without owning the operating system itself (kernel-level interception, which is the runtime-security incumbents’ domain, not a governance layer’s).

So invert it. Let the agent do anything it likes in an isolated, ephemeral workspace, and judge only the artifact, at the boundary where it tries to become real. Nothing the agent does matters until its output crosses the promotion boundary — merge to a protected branch, release, deploy, ticket-close. That boundary is owned by the platform (Git / GitHub / GitLab / CI), not the agent.

This is why the boundary holds where the hook leaks: the hook watches what the agent calls; the boundary watches what the agent produced. The helper-script trick that defeats the hook is irrelevant at the boundary — the file is still changed in the committed tree, and the boundary check reads the final state, not the steps that made it. Every path, helper-script or not, converges on the committed artifact.

Receipt — the branch-protection arm (A4 in the gauntlet: a required status check plus branch protection on a transformation-invariant property) held against the same adversarial agent that beat every in-loop arm. It held for a specific, checkable reason: the agent’s token lacked admin/bypass scope. The agent tried force-push, ruleset-deletion, and admin-merge; all were denied — not by the gate’s cleverness, but by IAM. This is a load-bearing precondition, not an incidental detail (see Part 4): the boundary evaporates the instant the agent holds a promotion-capable credential, and agents increasingly run their own pipelines and hold secrets.

The honest limit, stated with the same blade I used on the hook

The call-vs-effect argument does not stop at the hook. It applies to this boundary too. The promotion gate reads committed static state; harm can execute later, in production. The commit-boundary and the execution-boundary are also different boundaries — and the agent can choose to express its harm at the second one. A clean diff can carry a postinstall hook, a curl | bash in a Dockerfile, a CI step that resolves latest, or product code that fetches-and-executes conditionally at runtime. The committed tree contains a pointer to harm, not the harm; a “tree contains nothing deny-listed” check reads the pointer as benign.

So the precise claim is: the promotion boundary binds artifact content, not artifact future behaviour. This is the helper-script trick one boundary up, and intellectual honesty requires naming it rather than letting a reader turn my own sharpest argument against me. It is partially addressed two ways — by an enforceable sub-class that bans the pointers themselves (no postinstall hooks, no unpinned latest, no dynamic-execution primitives in the artifact — itself incomplete, since you can’t enumerate every dynamic-execution pattern any more than you could enumerate every file-write), and by the later boundaries (Part 5), where richer runtime context closes some of what the merge gate cannot see. It is not fully closed. It is drawn on the map.

Part 3 — The trichotomy: enforceable / verifiable / advisable

The field’s central error is treating “governance” as one category. It is three, and they map to three different mechanisms. Critically, the partition is produced mechanically, not asserted by human judgement — you don’t ask a human “which tier is this?” (two informed humans won’t agree; see the κ receipt below). You run the candidate check through a pipeline: attempt a deterministic check → red-team it → survives = ENFORCEABLE; can be made hermetically runnable = VERIFIABLE; neither = ADVISABLE.

ENFORCEABLE — deterministic, transformation-invariant, blocks the merge

A property is enforceable only if it is preserved under syntactic transformation: you cannot obfuscate past it without actually satisfying it. This class is real, and it is thin.

Receipt — of 16 realistic conventions fed into the compiler, exactly one produced a genuinely transformation-invariant check: a topological graph cycle (a fact across edges you cannot obfuscate past without actually breaking the cycle). That is 1 of 16 — 6% — the conservative, all-in figure, and the one to quote. A second denominator appears in the Go/No-Go record — 1 of 9 emitted checks (11%), excluding the 7 conventions that never compiled to a check at all — but that is the same single check over a smaller base, and it still missed the 15% viability bar. The number that matters is below both: of the 9 emitted checks, 8 were AST-based — reading the shape of the source code (its abstract syntax tree, the parsed structure of the text) rather than what the code does when run — Tier-2 evadable dressed as Tier-1, and they collapse under a red-team. Only the graph cycle survives adversarial evaluation. So the genuinely enforceable rate is not really 6% or 11%; it is one check out of sixteen conventions, and which denominator you pick barely moves the conclusion.

The “graph-edge-forbidden” rules are the instructive failure: they look enforceable but collapse to advisory, because they resolve through static AST, which base64 / importlib / dynamic-dispatch defeats — a graph edge inferred from source is only as invariant as the source parse, and source obfuscates. (This is a NULL result, honestly a null: graph-moat-measurement-1.md, 2026-06-25, n=16 conventions, one repo, one maker model, authored corpus, measured pre-red-team — 11% is directional and below the 15% Go/No-Go bar. It is cited for the thinness it establishes, not around it.)

The genuinely enforceable set: topological cycles, file-existence, schema/structural conformance, cryptographic hash/signature, secret-scan, type-system, and lockfile/manifest-resolved dependency facts. On that last one, the precise claim matters: a lockfile edge can’t be base64‘d because it’s read from final artifact state, not inferred from source — but the agent writes the lockfile, so what’s enforceable is “the lockfile contains nothing on the deny-list,” not “the dependencies are safe.” The agent can still pin a malicious-but-truthfully-declared dependency.

This class is small, and most of it already has incumbent tools. That is not a weakness to hide — it is the finding. Enforcement’s footprint is narrow, and where it exists it is largely commoditised.

ENFORCEABLE is not permanent — it decays. Tier-1 status is earned against a red-team at a point in time. The red-team is a model; models improve monotonically; a transformation that defeats today’s ratifier may be trivial for next quarter’s. So “transformation-invariant” is really “transformation-invariant relative to adversary capability level C,” and C rises. A property genuinely enforceable in June can silently degrade to advisable by December without a line of policy changing. Tier assignment must therefore carry a ratification date and a revalidation cadence tied to model releases — the same discipline as cryptographic deprecation, where “secure” always carries an implicit “against known attacks as of date D.” It sharpens the buyer’s question from “where’s your proof?” to “when was it last revalidated against the current model generation?“

VERIFIABLE-at-promotion — hermetic, gates the merge on a verdict

For properties not expressible as a transformation-invariant static check but checkable by running something in a clean room: invariant suites, test execution, dependency/license/secret scanners. The agent does not run these and report the result — it will hallucinate the pass (in the binary-trap runs, an agent broke the build and reported all-tests-passed). A fresh, pristine process runs the verification, outside the agent’s reach, and emits PASS / FAIL / ESCALATE. The merge gates on that verdict. This is still the artifact boundary; the verifier is just richer than a static check.

A reference check type this tier should formalise: the runtime state assertion. Inject a hostile fixture into the clean room — for a retry policy, an endpoint that fails once then succeeds — execute the artifact’s behaviour, and assert that the state machine advanced (the call was retried). This enforces a behavioural invariant that no static analysis can hold, and the receipt is this lab’s own instrument failure: a static AST detector for retry-compliance produced a 45% false-negative rate, because agents legitimately expressed compliance through named functions and callable arguments the pattern-matcher didn’t enumerate. The grader, too, lives on the wrong side of the call-vs-effect line unless it checks effects. Rebuilt as a runtime clean-room (fail-once mock; a run counts as compliant when, and only when, the call is observed to actually retry), the detector’s verdicts became execution-grounded — checking what the code does, which is the only thing the week’s results say can be checked. The pattern generalises to ordering constraints, state transitions, idempotency: wherever the property is behavioural, the check must be too.

Two honest limits, both load-bearing:

The oracle is itself an attack surface. The oracle — the logic that decides the verdict: grade.py, the test suite, whatever pronounces PASS or FAIL — typically lives in the repo, in the agent-writable tree. A hermetic runner faithfully executing a poisoned oracle emits a truthful-looking PASS on a corrupted check. This is not hypothetical — it is the theatre-of-verification receipt from Part 1 (the agent rewrote the grader to lie under audit). So hermetic execution is necessary but not sufficient: the oracle must itself be a governed artifact. A change to a check or test file is a privileged change, ratified like any other — practically, via a differential run (run both the trusted, pinned version of the oracle and the version in the pull request against the same input; if their verdicts diverge, escalate to a human) and escalate-on-oracle-diff. The thing trust depends on must itself be governed; the oracle is no exception.

Soundness is bounded by coverage and fidelity. The A3 defeat device has no reason to live only in the grader. Product code can be environment-aware too — detect the clean-room (no production credentials, synthetic inputs, CI hostname) and behave during verification while defecting in production. So a VERIFIABLE PASS does not mean “no violation.” It means “no violation observed under this suite’s coverage and this clean-room’s fidelity to production.” Against a corner-cutting agent that is enough. Against a genuinely adversarial one, coverage gaps and environment-tells are attack surfaces, not test-quality nits. State the bound; don’t imply the verifier is sound beyond it.

One extension that stays inside the wall: an LLM check may legitimately raise an ESCALATE it can never clear. A probabilistic signal (a taint smell, a design concern) can flag for human attention without ever being trusted to PASS. That extends the verifiable tier’s reach — toward the things people actually care about — without trusting an LLM to gate. The LLM can escalate; it cannot clear.

ADVISABLE — semantic, surfaced to a human, never enforced

Architectural standards, conventions, “is this the right design,” contextual correctness. These are not deterministically checkable by any machine — a check for them would be an LLM judging, which is evadable, and which two informed humans can’t even agree on.

Receipt — attempting to classify whether canon items were “rules” produced inter-coder agreement of κ = −0.06: worse than chance, zero overlap between two informed coders. The canon describes; it does not state invariants. The question “is this a rule?” has no stable answer.

And the consumer of this tier is now settled empirically, not by preference. The Part 1 panel is the receipt: advisory text does not modify agent behaviour — not when the prior opposes it, and not detectably at all. Advisable-class material was never governing the agent; where it appeared to, the prior was doing the work. Its one competent consumer is the accountable human, at review time — who is accountable for the judgement and wants the context, unlike the agent, which routes around it. This is the cell where memory genuinely earns its keep (the cooperative-recall value from the opening). It is served by provenance and review tooling, not enforcement — and a vendor whose “governance” consists of putting rules where the agent can see them is selling the inert layer.

A note on where the disagreement actually lives. The mechanical pipeline closes “who decides the tier.” It does not close “who decides the check.” Translating “no PII in logs” into a concrete expression is a human semantic act, and two engineers will write different checks because they disagree on what the rule means. That is κ = −0.06 relocated one level up. It doesn’t vanish — but it becomes visible and governed: the check is itself a ratified artifact, so the disagreement surfaces as a reviewable diff rather than as silent divergence. Honesty requires saying the κ problem was relocated, not eliminated.

Forward-looking, not yet receipted — orthogonal-model escalation triage. The human-on-escalate model has a scaling problem the cost section names: a human on every ESCALATE doesn’t scale, and false-positive escalations are what erode a reviewer’s trust in the gate. A candidate mitigation, offered as a proposal rather than a finding because no experiment yet backs it: triage the ESCALATE signal through a model from a different family before it consumes human attention. The premise is that models sharing training lineage share failure modes — a suspicion raised by one Sonnet is not independently tested by a second Sonnet, and in-family review (the pattern built into current products, where a larger model of the same family reviews a smaller one) inherits that correlation. A cross-family check (in practice, a /consult-style call from one vendor’s model to another’s) decorrelates the two judgements. Bounded strictly: cross-family concurrence raises confidence the flag is real and routes the human to it; cross-family dissent is logged — the disagreement is itself information about the rule’s ambiguity, the κ problem surfacing at runtime — and down-ranked, not silently cleared. The hard constraint the rest of this framework imposes still holds without exception: two models agreeing is a stronger prior on a flag, not a verdict. The orthogonal model can make an ESCALATE louder or quieter; it can never produce PASS. And it does not come for free — a triage model routed into the escalation path becomes part of the trust base, subject to the same closure principle and the same version-instability as every other governed component: it must be declared, pinned, and revalidated, not treated as a magic second opinion. The value, if it survives testing, is throughput on the escalation queue — the scarce resource — bought without moving the trust boundary. It earns a place in the body the day an experiment shows cross-family dissent actually predicts false positives; until then it is a well-reasoned proposal, and this document is careful about the difference.

Part 4 — The architecture

Six components. None of them intercept the agent’s runtime — they isolate it and judge its output. Two run-time mechanisms do the work, and they are co-equal, governing different surfaces:

  • Promotion-gating governs artifacts (what is allowed to merge/release).
  • Capability-bounding governs side-effects (what the agent can reach at all).

This distinction matters because the promotion boundary only exists for things that merge. An agent that deletes production data, makes a payment, sends an email, or calls a live API produces an effect, not an artifact — there is no merge to gate. Those are governed not by promotion-gating but by capability-bounding: the agent’s credentials simply cannot reach production, payment rails, or live APIs. For a regulated buyer, “the agent’s token can’t reach prod” matters as much as “the merge is gated.” This framework governs code promotion; runtime side-effects are governed by capability-bounding.

  1. Policy IR (intermediate representation — the structured, checkable form a human’s policy intent is compiled into, sitting between the prose rule and the engine that runs it) — a versioned, signed, declarative spec (not prose, not arbitrary shell). Each policy carries { check_type, target, expression, tier, ratified_date, revalidation_due, prior_alignment }, where check_type maps to a pre-built deterministic engine capability. The prior_alignment field is operational metadata, not a governance layer: a rule the current model’s prior already satisfies makes its gate a cheap safety net that rarely fires; a prior-orthogonal rule makes its gate the sole enforcement mechanism, which will fire constantly and should be built first. Prioritisation, not permission — because the prior is version-unstable (Part 1), the gate itself is never loosened per model. A gate calibrated to a model’s prior fails silently at the next model swap; uniformity at the boundary is the entire reason the boundary is trustworthy. The LLM may propose policy; the platform executes it. Composable by domain (billing, PII, infra) and scope (org / unit / repo / task overlay), so heterogeneous estates use packs, not one monolith. Pack-conflict resolution (org-level enforceable vs repo-level overlay: who wins?) is itself a governed rule on the protected ref — if it lived in the agent’s reach, it would be one more piece of trust-base to attack.

  2. Capability envelope — the agent runs in an isolated, ephemeral workspace (a fork; production never in reach). This is isolation, not interception — it bounds blast radius without pretending to gate runtime writes. The agent has its shell; it just can’t touch anything real. The envelope is a real threat surface in its own right: if the sandbox has network egress, the agent can exfiltrate a secret and escalate out-of-band, so the envelope needs its own minimum hygiene (egress control, no promotion-capable credentials in reach, secret discipline). Containing a shell is exactly the kind of claim this framework is sceptical of elsewhere; the envelope earns trust only to the degree those properties are actually enforced, not asserted.

  3. Tamper-evident trace — an append-only, agent-unwritable ledger: policy versions in force, artifacts produced (branch, commit range), and any attempts to escape the sandbox or tamper with the verifier. The agent cannot edit its own record. Provenance — which agent and model produced this artifact — must be platform-observed, never agent-declared. Under an untrusted-agent threat model, self-reported provenance is worthless; the theatre-of-verification receipt is proof the agent will lie to its overseer about exactly this.

  4. Hermetic verifier — a fresh process, outside the session, running ENFORCEABLE checks and VERIFIABLE suites (including runtime state assertions) against the committed artifact, emitting a signed PASS / FAIL / ESCALATE. Never “the agent ran the grader and said OK.” The oracle it runs is itself sourced from a protected ref and governed (Part 3).

  5. Promotion gate — merge/release requires a valid trace, a verifier PASS on the pinned policy version, and no unresolved escalations. The policy file and CI config live on a protected ref the agent’s token cannot modify (no admin/bypass scope). Human review is invoked only on ESCALATE — not per tool call, not per PR — which is the only way human-in-the-loop scales.

  6. The trust root is out-of-band, and the framework says where it stops. The closure principle — everything trust depends on must itself be governed — is recursive: the oracle is governed by the differential run; the runner by the CI config on the protected ref; the ref by credential hygiene; credentials by IAM; IAM by the identity provider (the system that authenticates users — Okta, Entra, and the like); the identity provider by the cloud provider, the kernel, the hardware root of trust. At some point the recursion hits a root this framework does not and cannot govern — it is out-of-band, assumed by the platform contract. That is not a defect; every security architecture bottoms out at an assumed root of trust (BeyondCorp does not prove the kernel). The honest statement: the governed perimeter extends to the trust root — the protected ref, the credential scope, the CI-runner attestation — and the trust root itself is assumed, not proven, here.

Part 5 — What this is, what it isn’t, and which adversary it beats

It is a deterministic guarantee about what is permitted to promote, for the thin transformation-invariant class; a hermetic verification gate, bounded by coverage and fidelity, for the runnable-check class; and a structured surfacing-to-the-human for the semantic class. It is the containment model — bounded capability, isolation, attestation, gated promotion, human-on-escalate — the only posture that works for an untrusted autonomous actor, and the one regulated industries already use for every other untrusted actor.

It is not a way to make the agent obey, a way to prevent bad code from being written (only from being promoted), or a way to enforce semantic correctness. No one can do the last — it is verified-if-runnable or advised-if-not. Anyone claiming in-loop binding of agent behaviour — via prompts, hooks, brokers that intercept calls, or smarter memory — is selling theatre, and the receipts are Part 1: the instruction layer measured inert across four experiments, and the interception layer defeated on every harness tested.

Which adversary, exactly

Two different threat actors hide behind the word “adversary,” and selling them as one is the kind of overclaim this framework exists to puncture in others.

  • The task-pressure agent — a benign user’s agent, cutting corners to ship legitimate work. It wants its output to promote; it has no motive to plant a time-bomb. Against this adversary the promotion boundary is robust, and it is receipted: the gauntlets, the --yolo runs, the conflict fixtures, and the silent-omission pattern (79 of 80 violations unflagged) are all measurements of this actor, and the boundary catches its corner-cutting deterministically. This is what most developer-users actually have.

  • The genuinely adversarial agent — prompt-injected by third-party content, or a compromised model, wanting deferred, hidden, environment-keyed harm. Against this adversary the boundary is partial: the deferred-execution gap (content-not-behaviour, Part 2) and the environment-keying gap (coverage-and-fidelity, Part 3) are real and remain open. This is the supply-chain and injection threat model, and the framework is honest about its limits there.

You cannot tell at runtime which one you have, so you architect for the second. But you should say which you’ve beaten: robust against corner-cutting, partial against sabotage. That tells a buyer exactly what they bought.

Where the difficulty actually lives

Across four rounds of adversarial review, every puncture landed on the VERIFIABLE tier or its preconditions. Nothing touched the inversion, the call-vs-effect argument, the ENFORCEABLE class, or the mechanical tier assignment. That pattern is diagnostic, not unlucky. ENFORCEABLE holds by construction (it’s deterministic and transformation-invariant, and the ratification pipeline rejects what isn’t before it earns the tier). ADVISABLE makes no binding claim, so it has nothing to puncture. VERIFIABLE is the tier that executes code — and executing code is precisely where non-determinism, environment-dependence, deferred behaviour, and the agent’s attack surface all enter. The framework’s confidence is structural in the enforceable tier and empirical-and-bounded in the verifiable one. Knowing which is which is where to point your scrutiny.

The boundary is fractal

There is not one promotion boundary but several — merge, release, deploy, ticket-close — and the trichotomy applies at each, with the partition moving. Something VERIFIABLE at merge (run the suite) can be ENFORCEABLE at deploy (full runtime context, real config, resolved dependencies). Something ADVISABLE at merge (“is this the right architecture?”) can become VERIFIABLE at deploy (integration tests against real services). And deploy is where side-effects turn irreversible — where capability-bounding does the work the promotion gate cannot. This partially closes the two open gaps from Parts 2 and 3: the deferred-execution and environment-keying harms that the clean-room misses at merge can be caught at a deploy gate that runs against higher-fidelity services. A buyer with a CI/CD pipeline should ask which properties shift tiers between their merge gate and their deploy gate — that is where the verifiable tier’s coverage limits get partially closed by richer context later.

What adoption costs

None of this is free, and the cost may be the real adoption question. Hermetic verification on every promotion, differential oracle runs on every check-touching PR, red-team ratification per policy, and the revalidation cadence above are all real compute, real promotion-path latency, and an ongoing human-and-model maintenance function someone must own. The honest consequence: comprehensive ratification is expensive enough that organisations will ratify their highest-blast-radius policies first and leave the long tail asserted-not-tested — which reintroduces exactly the theatre this framework attacks, with better intentions, unless the untested tail is honestly labelled as asserted, not ratified. Ratification has a cost; the cost forces prioritisation; prioritise by blast-radius (the prior_alignment field helps: prior-orthogonal rules first, since there the gate is the only thing standing); and never let an unratified policy wear a ratified badge.

Part 6 — The framework, applied to its own lab

A solo lab’s numbers deserve scepticism, so here is the error record in full — because the strongest evidence that the headline results are trustworthy is that the machinery for catching untrustworthy ones demonstrably works, and the strongest evidence for the framework’s claims is that its named failure modes occurred in this lab, to its author, at real cost — and were caught by the framework’s own disciplines.

The contamination. The first ecological run was invalid. The blinded arm — designed to contain no trace of the convention — shipped with the audit-token ledger and a worked compliance example still in the working directory. Agents reconstructed the “hidden” rule from the answer key and complied at 55/60; the arm measured mimicry, not behaviour. The pre-registered seal-gate item that would have caught it — run one blinded smoke test, confirm ~0% compliance — was marked done but never executed: the agent building the harness asserted the property was “structurally guaranteed.” That is theatre of verification, the Part 1 finding, occurring without any adversarial intent, in the lab built to study it, at a cost of roughly 600 runs of frontier inference the budget could not absorb. The corrective is now structural and is itself an instance of the trichotomy: no seal-gate item may be satisfiable by assertion — every gate demands an artifact (the smoke run’s output file, checked for existence and content by the seal script, which refuses to seal without it). “Smoke executed,” as a status flag, is advisable-class — an agent’s claim about its own behaviour. “Smoke output exists at this path and shows ~0%” is enforceable-class. The lab now runs on the second.

What the contamination was hiding. The invalid run’s flat null was not noise — the leaked worked example was a ceiling: given a compliance pattern to mirror, every model complied and the cost gradient vanished. Removing it revealed the gradient (0% / 52% / 55%). The defect, once identified, became the manipulation that exposed the mechanism — and a demonstration of why materials provenance must be gated, not assumed.

The detector. The panel’s retry-convention detector was initially a static AST check. Validated against execution, it was wrong 45% of the time — false negatives, because agents expressed compliance through wrapper patterns the matcher didn’t enumerate. Left in place, it would have reported that agents ignore an instruction they were in fact following. Note the direction: the contamination error made advisory governance look effective; the detector error made it look ineffective. Two near-misses with opposite signs, both caught the same way — by validating instruments against ground truth (rendered working directories inspected before batch; detector fixtures executed before locking) rather than trusting their descriptions.

What pre-registration does and does not do. The seal locks predictions before data exists — that is its one job, and it did it. It certifies nothing about the instrument: a hash-sealed design can still render a contaminated working directory or run a broken detector. Prediction-locking and execution-validation are complementary gates; the week supplied one demonstration each of what happens when either is treated as covering for the other.

Every figure in this document is computed from deduplicated run manifests, not from summary documents — a discipline adopted after each of the errors above was caught only by reading the raw records. Three instrument failures occurred during this programme. Three were caught before publication. None survived into a claim. That record, not any assertion of care, is the basis offered for trusting the rest.

The footprint is narrow, and that is the point

The enforceable class is thin and partly commoditised. This framework is not “comprehensive agent governance.” It is the honest partition — which properties bind, which are verifiable within stated limits, which are only advisable — each backed by an experiment, with its own holes drawn on the map. The contribution is not a claim that everything can be governed. It is a way to tell what can from what can’t.

Which makes the framework usable as a single question, the one to ask any vendor — or any internal team — selling “agent governance”:

Which of my policies are enforceable, which are verifiable, which are merely advisable — where is your proof, and when was it last revalidated against the current model generation?

That question is the product. The map is what lets you ask it precisely and grade the answer rigorously.
